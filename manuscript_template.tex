\begin{abstract}
I present a hidden Markov model (HMM) framework for longitudinal Crohn's disease monitoring with state-dependent laboratory sampling. The model combines daily Gaussian wearable summaries, intermittent log-normal laboratory biomarkers, and the laboratory-draw indicator in one likelihood; laboratory values contribute only on draw days. At each landmark, I distinguish an unconditional first-passage distribution with current-flare mass at day zero from a transient-conditional distribution whose mean, for any fixed parameter set, is bounded by that set's Remission- and Mild-state hitting-time means. Parameters are estimated without simulator state labels by Baum--Welch expectation maximization. A @@N_PARAM_MEMBERS@@-member responsibility-conditioned empirical-Bayes mixture propagates parameter variation; the event-history comparator uses a separate @@N_HAZARD_MEMBERS@@-member patient-bootstrap ensemble. Evaluation retains administratively censored landmarks and the probability tail beyond the observation window, using proper scores, calibration, coverage, and sharpness with seed- and patient-clustered uncertainty. Across @@N_SEEDS_WORD@@ independent simulations of @@N_TOTAL@@ patients each, the endogenous-sampling HMM obtained a censored log score of @@PROP_NLL@@ (95\% confidence interval @@PROP_NLL_CI@@), a four-horizon mean Brier score of @@PROP_IBS@@ (@@PROP_IBS_CI@@), and @@PROP_COV@@ coverage for nominal 90\% intervals. Comparator-minus-proposed differences for the otherwise identical HMM without the draw indicator were @@DIFF_NOLAM_NLL@@ in log score (@@DIFF_NOLAM_NLL_CI_ABS@@) and @@DIFF_NOLAM_IBS@@ in mean Brier score (@@DIFF_NOLAM_IBS_CI_ABS@@). The analysis supports the mathematical time-to-flare interface but does not establish a practically important gain from the draw indicator in this well-specified regime.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{C}{rohn's} disease is characterized by transitions among clinically quiescent, mildly active, and flare states. Wearable devices can provide dense physiological measurements, whereas laboratory biomarkers are comparatively accurate but intermittent. The timing of a laboratory panel can itself be informative: patients with greater disease activity may be sampled more often. A monitoring model should therefore use the draw indicator as an observation rather than treating every absent laboratory value as missing at random.

A clinician-facing output should also be more explicit than a per-day flare score. At study day $d$, the relevant quantity is a distribution over the waiting time until the flare state is first occupied at or after the landmark. This waiting time is zero for a current flare; it is not the time to a new flare episode after recovery. A distinction arises when the current filter assigns positive probability to the flare state. The unconditional waiting-time law contains an atom at zero; the law conditional on not currently being in flare does not. Their means are different, and only the latter is constrained to lie between the state-specific transient hitting-time means.

This study makes five contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It specifies one coherent likelihood in which fresh daily wearable observations are Gaussian, laboratory biomarkers are log-normal when observed, and absent laboratory values never enter the emission density.
\item It derives both the unconditional and transient-conditional first-passage distributions, including the day-zero mass and the finite-horizon survival tail.
\item It fits the latent states by expectation maximization (EM) rather than using the simulator's true states, and it propagates parameter variation by a transparent responsibility-conditioned, equally weighted parameter mixture.
\item It compares the endogenous-sampling hidden Markov model (HMM) with a no-draw-indicator HMM, a narrowly defined draw-stratified-emission HMM, and a discrete-time event-history model on the same time-to-flare output using censoring-aware proper scores and clustered uncertainty.
\item It supplies a one-command reproducibility pipeline, complete probability mass function (PMF) archives, serialized HMM and event-history ensembles, generated tables and figures, acceptance tests of the core invariants, and a verification script that regenerates the reported result tables from the archived patient- and landmark-level outputs.
\end{enumerate}

The scope is deliberately limited to a controlled simulation study. No claim of clinical validation is made.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Informative observation and dropout}
The distinction among missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR), and missing not at random (MNAR) is foundational in longitudinal analysis \cite{little_rubin,daniels_hogan}. Diggle and Kenward gave an early selection-model treatment of informative dropout in longitudinal data \cite{diggle}. Informative dropout models commonly connect a longitudinal process and an event process through shared latent structure. Bartolucci and Farcomeni developed a discrete-time event-history extension of latent Markov models with informative dropout \cite{bartolucci2015}, and later a shared-parameter continuous-time hidden Markov and survival model \cite{bartolucci2019}. Marino and Alf\`o used finite mixtures of hidden Markov models to accommodate longitudinal responses subject to dropout \cite{marino2020}. Pandolfi, Bartolucci, and Pennoni considered a continuous-response HMM that accommodates intermittent missingness under a MAR assumption and informative dropout through an absorbing state \cite{pandolfi2023}. These works are directly relevant: they demonstrate how observation or dropout processes can share latent disease structure and how EM recursions can estimate the resulting models. Speekenbrink and Visser treated state-dependent missingness in hidden Markov models directly, letting the probability that an observation is missing depend on the hidden state so that missingness itself carries information about the state \cite{speekenbrink2025}; the $\lambda_i$ factor used here is an instance of that construction, applied to daily laboratory-panel occurrence alongside wearable channels observed every day. Lange et al.\ modeled a multistate disease process jointly with informative observation times, in which the timing of clinical encounters depends on the disease process \cite{lange2015}. State-dependent missingness is therefore not claimed here as a new contribution. The target of the present framework is a rolling first-passage prediction over time to flare, obtained by composing the filtered state posterior with the absorbing-chain fundamental matrix, rather than a study-dropout event or the disease-process parameters that are the target in \cite{lange2015}. The conceptual relationship to shared-parameter and event-history models is close and I do not claim otherwise; the contribution is the coherent combination of intermittent state-dependent laboratory observation and a first-passage clinical interface within a single filtering recursion.

Mechanically, the Bernoulli draw process is modeled at every day and enters the HMM emission likelihood alongside the wearable and observed laboratory factors, so information from a lab-absent day still updates the posterior through the $1-\lambda_i$ term.

\subsection{Hidden-state disease progression and first-passage prediction}
Forward--backward filtering and Baum--Welch estimation are classical HMM methods \cite{rabiner,cappe}. Absorbing Markov-chain identities provide exact first-passage distributions and means through the transient matrix $Q$ and fundamental matrix $(I-Q)^{-1}$ \cite{kemeny}. Joint longitudinal--event modeling connects evolving biomarkers to event times through shared structure \cite{tsiatis}, while threshold-regression work treats event times as first passage of a stochastic process to a boundary \cite{lee_whitmore}. Related disease-progression work has used hidden-state and semi-Markov models to represent clinical deterioration and absorption risk \cite{alaa} and continuous-time disease progression \cite{liu_cthmm}. Alaa et al.~\cite{alaa} model clinical observation times as a marked Hawkes process modulated by the latent state, addressing the informative-observation phenomenon that motivates the $\lambda_i$ factor here, and compose the forward-filtered state posterior with absorption probabilities of the latent process. The present framework instead works in discrete time and targets the complete first-passage distribution, including its day-zero mass and finite-horizon tail, evaluated with censoring-aware proper scores.

\subsection{Evaluation of probabilistic event-time forecasts}
A predictive distribution should be evaluated by proper scoring rules, calibration, and sharpness rather than by discrimination alone. The Brier score has a standard survival-data extension \cite{graf1999}, and Gneiting and Raftery provide the general theory of proper scores and the calibration--sharpness principle \cite{gneiting2007}. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) remains useful for the secondary question of current-state classification, but it does not evaluate the claimed time-to-flare distribution. Longitudinal wearable studies have also reported autonomic changes associated with inflammatory-bowel-disease activity and physiological changes preceding flares \cite{hirten2021,hirten2025}; those findings motivate the monitoring setting but do not constitute validation of the present simulation model.

\section{Model}
\subsection{Latent dynamics and observations}
Let $S_d\in\mathcal S=\{\R,\M,\F\}$ denote the latent disease state on day $d$. The physiological process is a time-homogeneous Markov chain with initial distribution $\vect{\eta}$ and transition matrix $P=(P_{ij})$. The flare state is not physiologically absorbing; it is made absorbing only within the landmark-specific first-passage calculation of Section~\ref{sec:hitting}.

Each day produces a wearable vector $Y_d^{(w)}\in\mathbb R^p$, a laboratory-draw indicator $L_d\in\{0,1\}$, and, when $L_d=1$, a positive laboratory vector $Y_d^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb R_+^q$. The observed tuple is
\begin{equation}
O_d=\left(Y_d^{(w)},L_d,L_dY_d^{(\ell)}\right).
\end{equation}
Days are indexed from zero: $d\in\{0,1,\ldots,D-1\}$ for a $D$-day observation window, so that the remaining observed follow-up at landmark $d$ is $C_d=D-1-d$. The filtration $\mathcal F_d=\sigma(O_0,\ldots,O_d)$ contains only information available through day $d$.

\subsection{Coherent observation likelihood}
Conditional on $S_d=i$, the wearable channels are independent Gaussian summaries,
\begin{equation}
Y_d^{(w)}\mid S_d=i\sim\mathcal N(\vect{\mu}_i^{(w)},\operatorname{diag}[(\vect{\sigma}_i^{(w)})^2]),
\end{equation}
the draw indicator is Bernoulli,
\begin{equation}
L_d\mid S_d=i\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\lambda_i),
\end{equation}
and each positive laboratory channel is log-normal when observed,
\begin{equation}
\log Y_d^{(\ell)}\mid(S_d=i,L_d=1)
\sim\mathcal N(\vect{\nu}_i,\operatorname{diag}(\vect{\tau}_i^2)).
\end{equation}
The complete day-$d$ emission factor is
\begin{align}
 b_i(O_d)&=\phi_p\!\left(Y_d^{(w)};\vect{\mu}_i^{(w)},\vect{\sigma}_i^{(w)}\right)
 \lambda_i^{L_d}(1-\lambda_i)^{1-L_d} \notag\\
 &\quad\times\left[f_{\mathrm{LN}}\!\left(Y_d^{(\ell)};\vect{\nu}_i,\vect{\tau}_i\right)\right]^{L_d}.
 \label{eq:emission}
\end{align}
Figure~\ref{fig:graph} shows the resulting graphical structure. Thus, when $L_d=0$, the laboratory-density factor equals one. No forward-filled laboratory value is evaluated in the HMM likelihood. Last-observation-carried-forward values are used only as causal covariates in the separate event-history comparator.

\subsection{Filtering under endogenous sampling}
Let $\vect{\eta}$ denote the initial state distribution, with $\pi_{0\mid -1}=\vect{\eta}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:predict} applies for $d\ge 1$, and Equation~\eqref{eq:update} applies from $d=0$. Let $\pi_d(i)=\Prb(S_d=i\mid\mathcal F_d)$. The prediction and update are
\begin{align}
\pi_{d\mid d-1}(i)&=\sum_{j\in\mathcal S}\pi_{d-1}(j)P_{ji}\label{eq:predict},\\
\pi_d(i)&=\frac{b_i(O_d)\pi_{d\mid d-1}(i)}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal S}b_k(O_d)\pi_{d\mid d-1}(k)}\label{eq:update}.
\end{align}
On a lab-absent day, posterior odds between states $i$ and $j$ contain the factor
\begin{equation}
\frac{1-\lambda_i}{1-\lambda_j}.
\end{equation}
If $\lambda_i>\lambda_j$, laboratory absence shifts posterior odds away from state $i$. When all $\lambda_i$ are equal, the factor cancels and the model reduces to the no-draw-indicator HMM.

\section{First-Passage Prediction}\label{sec:hitting}
At every landmark day $d$, define the rolling waiting time
\begin{equation}
H_d=\inf\{n\ge 0:S_{d+n}=\F\}.
\end{equation}
For the first-passage calculation, reorder the states as $(\R,\M,\F)$ and replace the flare row by an absorbing row. Write
\begin{equation}
P^*=\begin{pmatrix}Q&\vect{r}\\\vect{0}^{\trans}&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad N=(I-Q)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Assume $\rho(Q)<1$, so the flare state is eventually reached from either transient state and $N=(I-Q)^{-1}$ is well defined. Here $Q$ is the $2\times2$ transient block and $\vect{r}$ is the transient-to-flare column. Let
\begin{equation}
\vect{q}_d=(\pi_d(\R),\pi_d(\M))^{\trans}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Unconditional law}
The complete landmark-specific distribution is
\begin{align}
\Prb(H_d=0\mid\mathcal F_d)&=\pi_d(\F),\label{eq:p0}\\
\Prb(H_d=n\mid\mathcal F_d)&=\vect{q}_d^{\trans}Q^{n-1}\vect{r},
\quad n\ge1,\label{eq:pn}\\
\Prb(H_d>h\mid\mathcal F_d)&=\vect{q}_d^{\trans}Q^h\vect{1}.\label{eq:tail}
\end{align}
The final expression is retained as a tail category in every finite numerical PMF. In particular, the @@N_DAYS@@-day observation horizon is never used to renormalize the predictive distribution.

The unconditional mean is
\begin{equation}
\E(H_d\mid\mathcal F_d)=\vect{q}_d^{\trans}N\vect{1}.
\label{eq:uncondmean}
\end{equation}
This mean can approach zero because it is multiplied implicitly by the posterior probability of still being in a transient state.

\subsection{Conditional law given no current flare}
When $\vect{q}_d^{\trans}\vect{1}>0$, the transient-conditional mean is
\begin{equation}
\E(H_d\mid\mathcal F_d,S_d\in\{\R,\M\})
=\frac{\vect{q}_d^{\trans}N\vect{1}}{\vect{q}_d^{\trans}\vect{1}}.
\label{eq:condmean}
\end{equation}
Let $m_{\R}=(N\vect{1})_{\R}$ and $m_{\M}=(N\vect{1})_{\M}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:condmean} is a convex combination of $m_{\R}$ and $m_{\M}$, so
\begin{align}
\min(m_{\R},m_{\M})
&\le \E(H_d\mid\mathcal F_d,S_d\in\{\R,\M\}) \notag\\
&\le\max(m_{\R},m_{\M}).
\label{eq:bound}
\end{align}
Proofs of Equations~\eqref{eq:uncondmean} and~\eqref{eq:bound} are given in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The conditional mean is mathematically undefined whenever the transient posterior mass is exactly zero. In the numerical implementation I additionally suppress the conditional mean and display it as a gap when the transient mass falls at or below $10^{-8}$; by Equation~\eqref{eq:bound} the ratio itself remains bounded as the transient mass decreases, but when the patient is almost certainly in flare it carries no practical information. The choice between ``undefined'' and ``suppressed below threshold'' is definitional, not a change to the underlying joint prediction: the unconditional law $\Prb(H_d=n\mid\mathcal F_d)$ and the day-zero mass $\Prb(H_d=0\mid\mathcal F_d)$ are unaffected.

\subsection{Parameter-mixture predictive law}
For parameter draws $\theta^{(0)},\ldots,\theta^{(B)}$, where $\theta^{(0)}$ is the EM estimate and $B=@@N_PARAM_DRAWS@@$ (Section~\ref{sec:paramunc}), the reported PMF is
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_d(n)=\frac{1}{B+1}\sum_{b=0}^{B}
 p_{\theta^{(b)}}(H_d=n\mid\mathcal F_d),
\end{equation}
with the filtering recursion and first-passage transformation recomputed for every draw. This can widen the predictive distribution when transition, emission, or sampling parameters are uncertain. The transient-conditional mean of the mixture is formed after marginalizing over draws, as the ratio of the draw-averaged numerator and denominator of Equation~\eqref{eq:condmean}. Equation~\eqref{eq:bound} holds for each member. Because the mixture mean is a weighted average of the member conditional means, with weights proportional to each member's transient posterior mass, it lies between the smallest and largest member hitting-time means; it need not lie between $m_{\R}$ and $m_{\M}$ of the EM estimate alone.

\section{Latent-State Estimation and Comparators}
\subsection{Baum--Welch estimation}
The main HMM is fit without access to simulator state labels. For training patient $p$, the forward--backward recursions produce
\begin{align}
\gamma_{pd}(i)&=\Prb(S_{pd}=i\mid O_{p,0:D-1}),\\
\xi_{pd}(i,j)&=\Prb(S_{pd}=i,S_{p,d+1}=j\mid O_{p,0:D-1}).
\end{align}
Transition-count sums run over $d=0,\ldots,D-2$, while emission sums run over $d=0,\ldots,D-1$.

The principal M-step updates are
\begin{align}
\widehat P_{ij}&=
\frac{\sum_{p,d}\xi_{pd}(i,j)+\alpha_{ij}}
{\sum_{p,d}\sum_k\xi_{pd}(i,k)+\sum_k \alpha_{ik}},\label{eq:Pupdate}\\
\widehat\lambda_i&=
\frac{\sum_{p,d}\gamma_{pd}(i)L_{pd}+\alpha_i}
{\sum_{p,d}\gamma_{pd}(i)+\alpha_i+\beta_i},\label{eq:lamupdate}\\
\widehat{\vect{\nu}}_i&=
\frac{\sum_{p,d}\gamma_{pd}(i)L_{pd}\log Y_{pd}^{(\ell)}}
{\sum_{p,d}\gamma_{pd}(i)L_{pd}},\label{eq:labupdate}
\end{align}
with analogous responsibility-weighted updates for Gaussian wearable means and variances and log-laboratory variances. Weak pseudocounts ($\alpha_{ij}=0.5$ for transitions and $\alpha_i=\beta_i=1$ for draw rates) prevent zero transition and draw probabilities. Initialization uses $K$-means on standardized training wearables; labels are re-sorted after every EM iteration and every parameter draw by increasing wearable-severity score, not by true states. The M-step also uses small numerical safeguards: a 0.5 pseudocount on the initial distribution, standard-deviation floors, laboratory updates only when a state's total draw-day responsibility exceeds 0.2, and draw rates clipped to $[10^{-4},1-10^{-4}]$. The observed-data log likelihood determines convergence: iteration stops when its change is at most @@EM_TOL@@ times one plus its absolute value, or after @@MAX_EM_ITER@@ iterations; at least three iterations are run.

\subsection{Parameter uncertainty}\label{sec:paramunc}
At the final EM solution, responsibility-weighted expected counts are used to construct a reproducible empirical-Bayes approximation. Initial and transition probabilities are drawn from Dirichlet distributions based on expected counts; draw rates are drawn from beta distributions based on expected draw and non-draw counts; independent Gaussian/log-Gaussian means and scales are drawn from weakly regularized normal--scaled-chi-square approximations. @@N_PARAM_DRAWS_WORD_CAP@@ draws plus the EM estimate are used for each HMM, for @@N_PARAM_MEMBERS@@ mixture members per model. Each primary HMM fit uses @@N_STARTS_WORD@@ observable-data initializations and retains every likelihood trace and selection diagnostic. This procedure propagates parameter variation but conditions on the final EM responsibilities; it is therefore not claimed to be an exact fully Bayesian posterior.

\subsection{Comparators}
Four models produce a PMF over the same $H_d$ outcome.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{HMM + draw model}: Equation~\eqref{eq:emission}, including $\lambda_i^{L_d}(1-\lambda_i)^{1-L_d}$.
\item \emph{HMM without draw model}: the same latent dynamics and value emissions, but the Bernoulli draw factor is omitted.
\item \emph{Draw-stratified-emission HMM}: wearable emission parameters are stratified by the currently observed draw status $L_d=0$ versus $L_d=1$, while one transition matrix and one filtered posterior are retained. As in the no-draw HMM, the Bernoulli draw factor is omitted, so draw status enters only through the stratified wearable parameters. This is a deliberately narrow selection-pattern comparator rather than a claim to represent every pattern-mixture formulation. Because the required posterior and transition matrix exist, Equations~\eqref{eq:p0}--\eqref{eq:tail} are applied directly.
\item \emph{Discrete-time event-history model}: a supervised comparator with two logistic components, both fit on training-set landmarks, taken every @@HAZARD_STRIDE_ORD@@ day, with ground-truth flare labels. Both components share the following landmark covariates: current wearables, seven-day wearable means and slopes, the draw indicator, last observed log-laboratory values, time since the last panel, and an explicit indicator that a first laboratory panel has become available. The hazard input additionally uses the lead-time basis defined below. Before a patient's first panel, log-laboratory features use the training-cohort median rather than a generating parameter. Both logistic components are $\ell_2$-regularized (inverse-strength $C=1.0$ for the current-state logistic, $C=0.5$ for the hazard logistic). The current-state logistic predicts the probability $g_0(x_d)$ that the patient is in Flare on the current day. The hazard logistic predicts a first flare at lead day $n$, conditional on remaining flare-free through lead day $n-1$ and not being in flare at the landmark:
\[
g_h(x_d, n) = \Prb(H_d = n \mid H_d \ge n,\ S_d \neq \F,\ x_d).
\]
Let $H_{\mathrm{train}} = @@HAZARD_HORIZON@@$ denote the training-horizon scale and $K = @@PMF_HORIZON@@$ the prediction-grid horizon. The hazard time basis is $\bigl(\log(1+n),\ \sqrt{n}/10,\ n/H_{\mathrm{train}}\bigr)$; it is extrapolated beyond the training horizon up to $K$. For each ensemble member and $1 \le n \le K$,
\[
\widehat p_d(0) = g_0(x_d),
\]
\[
\widehat p_d(n) = [1 - g_0(x_d)]\, g_h(x_d, n) \prod_{k=1}^{n-1}[1 - g_h(x_d, k)],
\]
\[
\widehat p_d(> K) = [1 - g_0(x_d)] \prod_{k=1}^{K}[1 - g_h(x_d, k)].
\]
Hazards are clipped to $[10^{-5}, 1-10^{-5}]$; by construction each row sums to one over the tail-inclusive grid, and a final normalization only removes floating-point rounding. Each fitted base component is accompanied by @@N_HAZARD_DRAWS@@ patient-bootstrap refits, giving @@N_HAZARD_MEMBERS@@ equally weighted members per component; ensemble predictions are the arithmetic mean of member PMFs. Only information available through day $d$ enters prediction; training labels are used only in fitting, never at inference.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Censoring-aware evaluation}
For landmark $d$, let $C_d=D-1-d$ be the remaining observed follow-up. If a flare is observed after $h_d\le C_d$ days, the censored logarithmic score is $-\log\widehat p_d(h_d)$. If no flare is observed before administrative censoring, the score is
\begin{equation}
-\log\Prb(H_d>C_d\mid\mathcal F_d),
\end{equation}
using the unrenormalized survival tail. Thus, patients without a flare inside @@N_DAYS@@ days remain in the analysis.

For $h\in\{7,14,30,60\}$, the fixed-horizon Brier score uses landmarks with complete $h$-day follow-up,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{BS}(h)=\frac1{N_h}\sum_{d:C_d\ge h}
\left[\widehat F_d(h)-\bm1\{H_d\le h\}\right]^2,
\end{equation}
where $\widehat F_d(h)=\sum_{n=0}^{h}\widehat p_d(n)$ is the predicted probability of a flare within $h$ days and $N_h$ is the number of the patient's landmarks with $C_d\ge h$; the score is computed for each patient and then averaged across patients. The reported four-horizon mean is the average of the four Brier scores. Thirty-day calibration is displayed graphically and summarized by slopes from a numerically ridge-stabilized logistic calibration model including an intercept. Slope confidence intervals use the hierarchical seed--patient bootstrap. Equal-tailed 90\% interval coverage is assessed against the separately simulated future latent path, with the $>@@PMF_HORIZON@@$ tail retained as an observed outcome category; predictive entropy is the primary sharpness summary. AUROC for current flare status is secondary.

For performance intervals, the bootstrap first resamples seeds and then resamples patients within selected seeds, preserving every repeated landmark for a selected patient. Paired model differences are calculated at matched seed--patient pairs before the same hierarchical bootstrap. The main performance intervals use @@PERFORMANCE_BOOTSTRAP@@ replicates; calibration intervals use @@CALIBRATION_BOOTSTRAP@@ replicates. The primary estimand includes every landmark, including a current flare represented by the explicit day-zero mass. Of the test landmarks, @@CURRENT_FLARE_FRACTION@@ are current-flare landmarks. Scores restricted to landmarks without a current flare are not reported: selecting landmarks on the realized current state, which is part of the predicted outcome, makes the restricted log and Brier scores improper for the unconditional law.

\section{Discussion}
Under the chosen state separability and draw rates, the explicit draw-indicator factor did not yield a statistically clear improvement over the otherwise identical HMM. This outcome is largely expected in the simulated regime: for every ordered pair of states, the per-day Kullback--Leibler divergence between the generating wearable emissions is @@KL_RATIO_MIN@@ to @@KL_RATIO_MAX@@ times that between the corresponding draw-indicator laws, so the wearable channels identify the state far more strongly than the timing of laboratory panels does. The regime is therefore a near-null setting for the draw indicator: the upper 95\% confidence limit of the log-score difference, @@NULL_NLL_UB@@, excludes a relative gain larger than about @@NULL_GM_PCT@@ in the geometric mean of the predictive probability assigned to the observed outcome. The posterior-odds contraction on lab-absent days (Appendix~\ref{app:contraction}) does not by itself imply a practically important gain in proper predictive scores. The gain may be larger when value emissions overlap more strongly, sampling contrasts are more extreme, or draw timing contains information not already captured by the wearable channels. Those regimes should be pre-specified and examined in a separate sensitivity study rather than inferred from the present experiment.

The draw-stratified-emission HMM also produces a legitimate time-to-flare PMF in the implemented form because it retains one transition matrix and one state posterior. Its similar proper-score performance illustrates why classification AUROC alone is insufficient for establishing the value of a new clinical interface. This comparator conditions wearable emissions on the observed draw stratum and leaves one common transition law; it is not a general pattern-mixture model. The event-history comparator performed less well here, but it is supervised and structurally different. Broader studies should include joint longitudinal--survival models and latent Markov models for informative dropout such as those in \cite{bartolucci2015,bartolucci2019,marino2020}.

\subsection{Limitations}
The study remains simulation-only and correctly specified for the principal HMM. State emissions are highly separated, producing near-ceiling state AUROC and limiting the realism of the classification task. The generating chain is flare-rich (stationary Flare probability @@STAT_FLARE@@), so absolute risks and calibration slopes should not be read as clinical flare frequencies. Only one Markov transition regime and one set of draw rates are used, and @@N_SEEDS_WORD@@ simulation seeds provide only a modest number of top-level uncertainty clusters. EM can converge to local optima. In every fit, the @@N_STARTS_WORD@@ observable-data starts reached final log likelihoods within @@START_SPREAD_BOUND@@ of one another; because all @@N_STARTS_WORD@@ starts are $K$-means initializations that differ only in random seed, this agreement is only weak evidence that EM avoided a poor local optimum. Under the stated relative stopping rule, every EM fit stopped after @@EM_ITER_MIN@@ to @@EM_ITER_MAX@@ iterations, well before the @@MAX_EM_ITER@@-iteration cap, so the reported solutions are converged to that tolerance. The archived traces show little remaining optimization: in every reported fit each log-likelihood increase was positive and at most @@EM_RATIO_MAX@@ times the previous one, the final update added at most @@EM_LAST_GAIN_MAX@@, and geometric extrapolation at each fit's largest observed ratio suggests about @@EM_REMAIN_MAX@@ more, against magnitudes of @@EM_ABS_LL_MIN@@ to @@EM_ABS_LL_MAX@@~$\times 10^{4}$. The responsibility-conditioned empirical-Bayes mixture is not a fully Bayesian posterior because state responsibilities are not redrawn jointly with parameters. The event-history uncertainty ensemble resamples training patients and refits both logistic components, but it remains a model-specific bootstrap rather than a joint Bayesian analysis. Equal-tailed 90\% intervals for a discrete predictive law contain at least 90\% predictive probability, so empirical coverage can exceed the nominal level (Section~\ref{sec:primary}). The fixed-horizon Brier analysis for all models uses landmarks with complete follow-up at the relevant horizon. The four-horizon mean therefore averages scores over horizon-specific eligible populations. Random censoring would require inverse-probability weighting or another censoring model. Finally, latent future continuation is available only because the data are simulated; real clinical calibration requires prospectively observed flare outcomes.

\subsection{Deferred external validation}
A real-data evaluation should be undertaken only after four conditions are met: individual-level data and code reproduce every count; repeated observations are analyzed with patient-clustered or mixed-effects methods; the transition matrix is estimated on the actual observation cadence rather than overlaying daily means on visit-scale data; and the flare outcome is independently adjudicated and is not defined using the same measurement serving as the principal predictor. Scheduled study blood draws should not be interpreted as symptom-triggered clinician ordering without additional evidence. Until then, external data may motivate the model but cannot validate the time-to-flare PMF.

\section{Conclusion}
A coherent hidden Markov model with endogenous laboratory sampling can generate a complete first-passage distribution at every landmark day. The clinically interpretable output requires an explicit day-zero mass, an unrenormalized survival tail, and a clear distinction between unconditional and transient-conditional means. When the model is fit without oracle states and evaluated with censoring-aware proper scores, the simulation study supports the mathematical interface but finds no clear predictive benefit from the laboratory-draw indicator in the tested regime. The appropriate next step is independent reproduction of the tagged pipeline, followed by pre-specified robustness experiments and prospective clinical validation.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
\subsection{First-passage distribution and mean}
For $n\ge1$, a first hit at $d+n$ requires the chain to remain transient for $n-1$ steps and move to flare on the next step, giving $\vect{q}_d^{\trans}Q^{n-1}\vect{r}$. The probability of no hit through $h$ steps is $\vect{q}_d^{\trans}Q^h\vect{1}$. Summing the tail probabilities and using the Neumann series,
\begin{align}
\E(H_d\mid\mathcal F_d)
&=\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\Prb(H_d>h\mid\mathcal F_d)\\
&=\vect{q}_d^{\trans}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}Q^h\right)\vect{1}
=\vect{q}_d^{\trans}(I-Q)^{-1}\vect{1}.
\end{align}
The day-zero mass contributes zero to the mean but reduces $\vect{q}_d^{\trans}\vect{1}$, allowing the unconditional mean to approach zero.

\subsection{Transient-conditional bound}
Let $w_i=\pi_d(i)/(\pi_d(\R)+\pi_d(\M))$ for $i\in\{\R,\M\}$. Then $w_i\ge0$, $w_{\R}+w_{\M}=1$, and Equation~\eqref{eq:condmean} equals $w_{\R}m_{\R}+w_{\M}m_{\M}$, proving Equation~\eqref{eq:bound}.

\subsection{Posterior-odds contraction on a lab-absent day}\label{app:contraction}
When $L_d=0$, Equation~\eqref{eq:emission} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\pi_d(i)}{\pi_d(j)}=
\frac{1-\lambda_i}{1-\lambda_j}
\frac{\phi_p(Y_d^{(w)};\vect{\mu}_i^{(w)},\vect{\sigma}_i^{(w)})}
{\phi_p(Y_d^{(w)};\vect{\mu}_j^{(w)},\vect{\sigma}_j^{(w)})}
\frac{\pi_{d\mid d-1}(i)}{\pi_{d\mid d-1}(j)}.
\end{equation}
Holding the remaining parameters and the predictive prior fixed, omitting the draw factor removes the first ratio. Thus, if $\lambda_i>\lambda_j$, endogenous sampling contracts the posterior odds for $i$ relative to $j$ by a factor strictly between zero and one.

\section{Acceptance Tests}\label{app:tests}
The tagged release must satisfy the following assertions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every landmark and model, $\sum_{n=0}^{@@PMF_HORIZON@@}\widehat p_d(n)+\widehat p_d(>@@PMF_HORIZON@@)=1$ to numerical tolerance.
\item For every HMM parameter draw, the conditional mean is either suppressed (transient mass at most $10^{-8}$) or lies between the two state-specific means.
\item Changing a stored laboratory value on a day with $L_d=0$ leaves the HMM likelihood unchanged.
\item The simulator draws a new wearable error vector on every patient-day.
\item Training, validation, and test patient identifiers are disjoint.
\item HMM estimation uses only wearable values, laboratory values when drawn, and draw indicators. Simulator state arrays are not read by HMM initialization, estimation, or filtering. They are used to define evaluation outcomes and by the separately identified supervised event-history comparator during training.
\item Censored log scores use the survival tail and retain patients with no observed flare.
\item Draw-stratified-emission and no-draw HMMs are transformed to the same time-to-flare outcome as the proposed HMM.
\item Hierarchical confidence intervals resample seeds and patients, never individual patient-day rows as independent observations.
\item Generated tables agree with the manuscript to the displayed precision when independently regenerated from the archived patient-level files.
\item The event-history pre-panel laboratory reference is computed from training data and accompanied by an explicit panel-history indicator; no generating laboratory parameter enters the comparator.
\item Every seed archives a complete PMF tensor, every fitted model object, the selected and non-selected EM starts, and all patient-bootstrap hazard members.
\end{enumerate}
